      Method and sources

      How to read these numbers

      Records, not people

      ISTAT publishes records of people reported, arrested or detained by the police: one per person per crime. Someone reported for a robbery and for the injuries inflicted during that robbery appears twice, and someone reported several times in the year counts each time. The numbers do not say how many young people are involved, but how many times a minor was named as the person who committed a crime.

      Reported does not mean guilty

      A record is the start of proceedings, not a conviction. For minors the juvenile court decides, and it can close the case with a period of probation or on the grounds that the offence is too minor to matter. Children under 14 cannot be held criminally responsible, but they can still be recorded.

      Why a share and not a rate per resident

      This page does not calculate how many minors are recorded per 100,000 young residents: it looks at what share of all the people reported or arrested for the same crime are minors. The share tells you whether a crime has become “younger”; it does not tell you whether young people commit more crimes than adults, and it also depends on how many crimes adults commit and on how many young people live in a region.

      Two time windows

      Records of minors are published from 2004, those for the individual types of theft and robbery from 2006. Records for all people, needed to work out the share, are available here from 2014. The years 2020 and 2021 were the Covid years: closed schools and restrictions on movement pushed almost every number down.

      What is left out

      Crimes that nobody reports, and those whose offender is never found: for thefts, more than nine in ten in Italy (see the deep dive on unknown offenders). If a group of young people is identified more easily than adults, for example because they act on the street and in groups, its share of the people reported or arrested rises even when the number of crimes stays the same.
